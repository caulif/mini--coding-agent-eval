How can a quality requirement become an executable, interpretable set of graders?

Identify the requirement, find the evidence, then choose programmatic, model, or human assessment.

Requirements, evidence, and judgments

“Preserves existing behavior” calls for regression tests and their results. “The explanation matches the actions” calls for a final response and ordered tool records. Choosing the judge after identifying the evidence helps avoid substituting an easy-to-measure score for the quality that matters.

Programmatic grading

Directly executable conditions are best checked directly. For knapsack, exhaustive search over small inputs can produce expected optimum values for comparison with candidate code. Acceptance cases should trigger the defect and cover nearby behavior; one example can let a special-case patch slip through.

Passing establishes the conditions covered by those tests. Uncovered inputs, performance regressions, and unnecessary complexity require other evidence. The grader itself needs checks: the buggy version should fail, a known correct repair should pass, and plausible wrong repairs should be rejected.

Model and human grading

Open-ended statements and maintainability are difficult to express entirely as exact rules. An LLM judge can apply a clear rubric, but its output remains a measurement affected by the materials, wording, and model. Humans help define criteria, resolve disagreements, and calibrate errors. A stronger judge cannot resolve ambiguity in the criteria themselves.

A patch-quality rubric might ask whether changes introduce irrelevant branches, duplicate existing logic, or break an explicit interface, with references to diff locations. Asking only “Is this good code?” rarely produces a stable judgment. I implement functional grading and a verification-claims judge here: one executes checks, the other can be inspected against a trace. Maintainability needs diffs, context, and separate calibration samples, so it remains an extension.
